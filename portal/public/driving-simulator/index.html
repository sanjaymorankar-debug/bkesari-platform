Driving simulator

Driver training on a simulator: a way to practise the situations that are genuinely unsafe to rehearse on a real road.

Why a simulator and not just more road time

Road practice can only teach what happens to come up. A learner might finish a course having never met a blown tyre, a wet corner taken too fast, or a child stepping out from between parked vehicles — the situations that most need a trained response.

A simulator can put those in front of a driver deliberately, repeat them until the response is automatic, and do it without risk to anyone. It can also be stopped mid-incident and replayed, which a real road cannot.

What it does well

  • Hazards on demand. Emergency braking, skids, sudden obstacles and brake failure, rehearsed as often as needed.
  • Conditions that cannot be arranged. Night, fog, heavy rain and glare, without waiting for the weather or the season.
  • Repetition without cost. The same junction a dozen times over, with no fuel burnt and nothing to damage.
  • Mistakes made safely. Getting it wrong is how the lesson lands, and here getting it wrong is free.
  • Calm first attempts. Controls and clutch work can be learnt before traffic is added to the problem.

What it does not replace

A simulator does not reproduce the full feel of a real vehicle, real road surfaces, or the judgement of other drivers around you. It is preparation for road practice and a way to drill specific situations — not a substitute for supervised driving or for the legal requirements of a licence.

Who it tends to suit

  • New drivers, building the basics before facing traffic.
  • Returning drivers, rebuilding confidence after a long gap or after a collision.
  • Commercial and fleet drivers, where refresher training on hazards is part of the job.
  • Anyone nervous about a specific situation — a particular junction, night driving, reversing — who wants to practise just that, repeatedly.
